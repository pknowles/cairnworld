# The Mill in Flood

*A short Cairn scenario about a trapped miller, a rising millpond, a jammed floodgate, and a beaver that has made its home in exactly the wrong place.*

## Premise

Heavy rain has swollen the stream above the village mill.

Normally, excess water from the millpond escapes through a **waste channel** beside the mill. Its gate is now jammed shut by the nest of a territorial beaver.

Rather than approach the animal, the miller tried to lower the pond by sending more water through the **millrace** and waterwheel.

The gate-control mechanism failed under the unusual load.

The miller is now pinned beneath the broken control beam. The damaged mechanism is also preventing the mill sluice from opening fully.

If the miller is simply pulled free, the gate will drop open.

A large volume of water will rush through the millrace. The wheel and machinery may fail violently, the lower mill will flood, and the overflowing tailrace may threaten the lowest parts of the village.

Meanwhile, water from the rising pond is already entering the mill.

Dozens of sacks of grain are stored on its lower floor.

There is no single correct solution.

---

# What Is Happening

The millpond has two outlets.

```text id="mill-layout"
                    swollen stream
                         ↓
                  ┌─────────────┐
                  │  MILLPOND   │
                  └──────┬──────┘
                         │
             ┌───────────┴───────────┐
             │                       │
        WASTE CHANNEL            MILLRACE
        flood bypass                 │
             │                       │
        [jammed gate]          [mill sluice]
             │                       │
          beaver                  waterwheel
             │                       │
             └──────────┐     ┌──────┘
                        ↓     ↓
                      stream / village
```

### The waste channel

This is the proper route for excess floodwater.

Its gate is stuck almost completely shut.

A beaver has built a dense nest around the sheltered gate chamber. Branches, reeds, mud, discarded cloth, and other material have become tangled through the gate and its guides.

Opening the gate properly requires clearing enough of this material for it to move.

The beaver considers the chamber its home.

### The millrace

The miller attempted the obvious alternative: use the millrace to drain the pond.

He opened its sluice much farther than normal.

The resulting force overloaded the wooden gate-control mechanism. A pivot or retaining pin failed and the heavy control beam dropped across him.

The beam now performs two accidental functions:

* it pins the miller;
* it keeps the gate from opening farther.

Moving it carelessly solves the first problem and creates a much larger one.

---

# Harl's Secret

The miller is not telling the characters everything.

The mill has an emergency means of separating the waterwheel from the main drive machinery.

A heavy wooden **sacrificial coupling** in the drivetrain can be knocked free.

It exists for exactly this kind of extreme overload.

If released:

* the millstones and internal gearing are disconnected from the wheel;
* the mill sluice can be opened farther with much less danger of the drivetrain tearing itself apart;
* substantially more water can be sent through the millrace;
* the pond can be lowered much faster.

But the wheel itself may overspeed, lose paddles, tear free from its axle, or damage the wheelhouse.

The mill could be unusable for weeks or months.

Harl knows this.

He does not mention it.

Instead, almost as soon as the characters reach him, he tells them:

> “You have to clear the bypass. Don't touch the wheel mechanism.”

His advice is not false.

Clearing the bypass is the safest way to preserve the entire mill.

But it is not the only way to save him or lower the pond.

Harl is concealing an alternative because destroying the wheel could ruin him.

He has spent years maintaining the mill. It is his livelihood, his status in the village, and probably most of what he owns.

He genuinely believes there is still enough time to save everything.

He may be wrong.

---

# Beginning the Scenario

The characters arrive during heavy rain.

Someone from the village has run for help.

The situation can be explained in one sentence:

> “The pond is overflowing, the waste gate won't open, and the miller is trapped inside.”

The characters need no further history.

## What they immediately see

At the mill:

* water pours over parts of the pond embankment;
* the millrace is running unusually hard;
* water is ankle-deep around the mill's lower entrance;
* villagers are carrying sacks of grain upstairs;
* the wheel turns much faster than normal;
* shouting can be heard from inside.

Nobody outside knows exactly why the waste gate is stuck.

Harl does.

---

# 1. The Lower Mill

Water runs across the floor toward the tailrace.

About **thirty heavy sacks of grain** remain here.

Each requires meaningful effort to move. A character can carry one sack at a time awkwardly, or two people can move one quickly.

The village cannot simply evacuate all of it at once.

### Useful things here

* several stout timbers;
* rope;
* iron pry bars;
* axes and mallets;
* sacks of grain;
* empty sacks;
* chains used for lifting millstones;
* wooden wedges;
* a hand winch;
* access to the wheel machinery.

Players should be encouraged to treat these as ordinary physical objects, not predefined puzzle pieces.

### The drivetrain

Anyone who deliberately examines the machinery can see that one section of the drive is connected by an unusually large wooden key or coupling.

It is clearly designed to be removable.

No roll is required to notice this if someone inspects the mechanism.

A character familiar with machinery can infer its likely purpose immediately.

Others may need to:

* ask Harl;
* ask an experienced villager;
* experiment carefully;
* inspect how the load transfers through the gearing.

If confronted, Harl initially minimizes its significance.

> “That's not for this. Leave it alone.”

If pressed, he eventually admits what it does.

### Moving the grain

The grain is not a decorative background detail.

It can be saved.

Players may decide to carry sacks upstairs, outside, or onto higher ground before doing anything irreversible to the mill.

This is a legitimate strategy.

If enough grain is moved to safety, the party can later choose to sacrifice the mill with far less concern about losing the village food supply.

That creates a coherent alternative plan:

> move grain → free Harl → allow or force the sluice open → sacrifice the mill

Harl understands this immediately if he sees them carrying sacks away in quantity.

And he does not like it.

### Harl's response to moving grain

At first he argues honestly:

> “Leave the sacks. Clear the bypass first.”

If they continue:

> “You don't have time to move all of that.”

If they make it clear they intend to clear the floor before freeing him, he becomes increasingly alarmed.

He may say:

> “If you let that gate go, the tailrace will overflow.”

That is possible.

He may then overstate the danger:

> “You'll flood the houses below.”

This is not necessarily true.

The real likely consequences are more limited:

* the wheelhouse may flood;
* the mill yard may take water;
* the tailrace may overflow locally;
* the wheel and lower structure may be badly damaged.

Harl knows the risk is serious, but he exaggerates it because he wants to preserve the mill.

If challenged, he insists the party is gambling with other people's lives.

He may genuinely believe that.

### Rising water

The water is currently shallow.

As the situation worsens:

1. sacks on the floor begin soaking;
2. moving around becomes difficult;
3. water reaches machinery and storage bins;
4. the lower floor becomes dangerous to occupy.

Do not advance this on a rigid turn counter.

Advance it when the characters spend substantial time, repeatedly fail at something, retreat to reconsider, or otherwise allow the situation to deteriorate.

---

# 2. The Gate Mechanism

Harl lies beneath a heavy timber arm connected to the millrace sluice.

He is conscious, frightened, wet, and in considerable pain.

He immediately warns anyone approaching him:

> “Don't lift it. The beam's holding the gate.”

He can explain the immediate mechanics plainly.

The waste gate should have been opened to lower the pond.

It would not move.

He knew something had been nesting around it for several days and had avoided clearing it because the beaver became aggressive whenever anyone approached.

With the rain worsening, he tried to compensate by opening the mill sluice wider.

The mechanism failed.

He does **not** initially explain that the wheel can be deliberately disconnected and sacrificed.

## The immediate problem

Simply lifting the beam is easy enough with several strong characters or leverage.

Doing so **without supporting the gate mechanism** allows the mill sluice to open fully.

Harl believes this could wreck the mill and cause downstream flooding.

With the drivetrain still engaged, he is probably correct.

### Securing the gate

There is no prescribed solution.

Reasonable ideas include:

* brace the mechanism with timbers;
* take its weight with rope and the hand winch;
* wedge the gate itself;
* improvise a new pivot;
* chain the control arm to structural timbers;
* gradually shift the load;
* lower or raise the gate by another means.

A sound plan that clearly supports the load should work.

Only call for a save when the characters perform something genuinely risky under pressure.

---

# Harl

**Harl, Miller**
6 HP, 1 Armor, STR 12, DEX 9, WIL 14

Harl is practical, stubborn, frightened, and extremely protective of the mill.

It is not merely his property.

The village depends on it.

He will accept:

* lost grain;
* temporary damage;
* personal risk;
* killing the beaver if necessary.

He strongly resists deliberately sacrificing the waterwheel.

## What Harl wants

In order:

1. clear the waste gate;
2. lower the pond safely;
3. secure the mill sluice;
4. free him;
5. preserve as much grain and machinery as possible.

From his perspective, this is the proper solution.

He is not wrong.

The problem is that it may take too long.

## What Harl conceals

If the characters ask whether there is any other way to move more water, he initially says:

> “Not without wrecking the mill.”

That is technically true.

If they ask whether wrecking the mill would work, he becomes evasive.

> “There's no need. Clear the bypass.”

If they discover the sacrificial coupling themselves, he becomes openly alarmed.

> “No. Don't touch that.”

If they propose knocking it free, he argues that:

* the wheel may tear itself apart;
* the wheelhouse may be damaged;
* the mill could be useless for months;
* the village will still need flour after the storm.

All of these are legitimate concerns.

But he also knows that sacrificing the wheel could substantially reduce the immediate danger.

## If the party starts saving grain first

This is when Harl's concealment becomes most active.

He realizes that once the grain is safe, the strongest argument for preserving the mill weakens.

He begins trying to stop the plan.

His escalation can be:

1. **Reasonable objection**
   “You're wasting time.”

2. **Technical warning**
   “The wheel won't survive.”

3. **Exaggeration**
   “The tailrace will overflow.”

4. **Lie by implication**
   “You'll flood the lower houses.”

5. **Direct interference**, if free and desperate
   He may seize a tool, block access to the coupling, or demand villagers stop the party.

Harl should not become cartoonishly evil.

His lies are rooted in fear, self-interest, and a real belief that the mill can still be saved.

## If the party tries anyway

Harl may:

* beg them not to;
* demand another attempt at the bypass first;
* ask villagers to stop them;
* seize a tool if he is free;
* physically stand between them and the coupling.

He does not suddenly become murderous.

If the danger becomes unmistakably immediate, especially if lives are clearly about to be lost, his position can change.

He may finally say:

> “Do it.”

Exactly when that happens depends on events.

---

# 3. The Waste Gate

A short channel carries floodwater around the mill.

At its entrance is a stout wooden gate that should now be mostly open.

It has moved only a few inches.

The cause is apparent upon inspection.

A **beaver** has built a dense nest in the sheltered gate chamber.

Its construction is partly what jammed the mechanism.

The nest contains:

* reeds;
* branches;
* grass;
* cloth;
* rope scraps;
* bits of sacking;
* mud.

Some material is tightly wedged around the gate.

Removing the obstruction means disturbing the nest.

## The beaver

The animal does not immediately attack.

It:

* watches;
* slaps its tail;
* retreats deeper into the chamber;
* charges or bites if cornered;
* becomes aggressive if someone begins tearing apart the nest within reach.

It wants intruders gone.

**Beaver**
5 HP, STR 12, DEX 12, WIL 8
Bite (d6)

If badly injured, it flees if an escape remains.

It does not fight to the death for no reason.

### Grain

The beaver will eat grain.

A handful may attract its attention.

A sack placed some distance away is a serious inducement.

More grain can make progressively more ambitious attempts to lure it away plausible.

The result should depend on circumstances rather than a fixed price such as “one sack automatically solves the encounter.”

The grain belongs to the village.

With water already threatening the stores, using it as bait has a visible cost.

This keeps the animal encounter tied to the same resource already under threat.

### Other approaches

Characters might:

* frighten it out;
* lure it with grain;
* trap it;
* block it away from the gate;
* drive it into the water;
* capture it;
* kill it;
* work around it;
* dismantle the gate from the other side.

A clever solution that removes the animal's ability or reason to interfere should not require a save.

---

# 4. The Wheel

The wheel is taking much more water than it normally would.

It groans under the load.

The mill's machinery provides another possible answer to the flood.

Harl does not want the party to use it.

## The sacrificial coupling

The wheel drives the mill through a deliberately replaceable wooden coupling.

Knocking it free disconnects the wheel from the millstones and much of the internal gearing.

This does **not** magically make the millrace safe.

It does make it possible to send substantially more water through the race without the entire drivetrain resisting the wheel.

The wheel may then:

* overspeed;
* shed paddles;
* damage its axle;
* tear loose;
* smash part of the wheelhouse.

The millrace and tailrace may also overflow locally.

But the pond can be drawn down much faster.

This can make freeing Harl and preventing a dam breach dramatically easier.

## Sacrificing the wheel

The characters may:

* knock out the coupling;
* disengage additional gearing;
* open the mill sluice farther;
* let the wheel run until it fails;
* deliberately damage paddles to reduce resistance;
* break parts of the wheelhouse if they need a clearer path for water.

Do not prescribe exactly how destruction must happen.

The important distinction is:

**preserving the mill limits how aggressively the millrace can be used.**

Sacrificing machinery removes that constraint.

## Consequences

Depending upon what they do:

* the wheel may need replacement;
* the axle may be damaged;
* gears may need repair;
* part of the wheelhouse may be wrecked;
* the mill may remain unusable for weeks or months.

The stored grain can still be eaten.

But without a working mill, turning it into flour becomes much harder.

This is therefore a serious loss to both Harl and the village.

---

# The Pressure

The scenario has three things worsening simultaneously.

## The pond

Water continues entering faster than it can leave.

Overtopping begins to erode the embankment.

If ignored long enough, part of the dam may fail.

A true breach is disastrous and should represent the scenario reaching its worst state, not an arbitrary surprise.

Signs should come first:

* muddy water begins cutting channels across the crest;
* pieces of bank slump away;
* cracks appear;
* villagers report the water rising behind it.

## The miller

Harl is pinned and injured.

Initially he can wait.

As time passes he becomes weaker, colder, and less able to help.

If the beam shifts, his condition may worsen quickly.

## The grain

Water rises through the lower mill.

The characters can see sacks being lost one row at a time.

This provides a cost for delay without creating an artificial countdown.

---

# Likely Outcomes

Do not treat these as branches the players must choose.

They are examples of consequences that follow naturally from the situation.

## Clear the waste gate

Probably the best outcome for preserving the mill.

Costs may include:

* time;
* confronting the beaver;
* injury;
* destroying its nest;
* grain used to lure it away.

Once enough water flows through the bypass, pressure on the millrace can be reduced and Harl can be freed more safely.

## Secure the mill gate first

This can free Harl quickly.

It does not solve the rising pond.

The characters gain another capable person but lose time elsewhere.

Harl immediately wants to help save the mill.

## Kill the beaver

Fast and straightforward if combat goes well.

Combat may also:

* injure characters;
* waste precious time;
* leave someone unable to perform heavy work later.

The animal is not evil and is only defending its home.

The scenario imposes no supernatural punishment or moral score for killing it.

## Lure the beaver away

Potentially safer.

It sacrifices some of the village's grain while other grain is already being ruined.

The players decide whether this cost is worthwhile.

## Move the grain, then sacrifice the mill

This is a strong emergent strategy.

The party may decide:

* preserve the food first;
* accept the loss of the mill;
* free Harl;
* let the millrace take the flood.

The plan is costly because moving the grain takes time.

It also provokes Harl's strongest opposition.

If executed successfully, it may preserve:

* most of the grain;
* Harl's life;
* the dam;
* the lower village.

The likely price is the wheel and perhaps much of the mill machinery.

## Sacrifice the wheel immediately

This can:

* rapidly increase outflow;
* lower the pond;
* reduce the risk of dam failure;
* make it much safer to free Harl.

The cost is the mill and potentially some grain still inside.

This option should become more attractive as conditions worsen.

Harl's resistance makes the decision socially difficult, but he cannot alter the physical facts.

## Move the grain without sacrificing the mill

This preserves resources but does nothing about the cause of the emergency.

Spending people and time here means not spending them elsewhere.

It may nevertheless be entirely rational if the players believe some loss is unavoidable.

---

# Incomplete Knowledge

The scenario works even if the players never discover Harl's concealment.

If they follow his advice and successfully clear the bypass, they may save everything.

The hidden information matters because it changes the apparent balance of risk.

Initially, the party may believe:

> Clear the bypass or suffer an uncontrolled mechanical failure.

After inspecting the drivetrain, they may realize:

> There is another option: deliberately sacrifice the wheel to gain control of the flood.

If they also realize the grain can be removed first, the choice changes again:

> We may be able to save Harl and the food by sacrificing the mill.

That is the point where Harl's incentives come into direct conflict with the party's risk assessment.

The hidden information should feel like discovering a **new tradeoff**, not uncovering the correct answer.

## Ways the secret can emerge

Do not force a reveal.

The players can discover it by:

* inspecting the drivetrain;
* asking why the coupling is removable;
* questioning another person who knows the mill;
* pressing Harl about alternatives;
* moving grain and noticing his reaction;
* proposing destruction themselves and observing how strongly he resists.

A player who never looks for another option may never learn it.

That is acceptable.

---

# Referee Principles

## Give physical information freely

The interesting part is choosing what to do with facts, not discovering basic facts through rolls.

If characters inspect the gate mechanism, explain what is supporting what.

If they inspect the waste channel, they find the nest.

If they study the water, describe where it is flowing and where it is backing up.

If they inspect the drivetrain, show them the removable coupling.

Do not hide physical evidence merely because Harl is hiding its significance.

## NPCs may conceal motives, not reality

Harl can:

* omit;
* minimize;
* exaggerate;
* argue;
* frame risks in his favor.

He should not make absurd technical claims that inspection immediately disproves.

His best deception is selective truth.

> “Opening that gate farther could wreck the mill.”

True.

What he leaves out is:

> Wrecking the mill may now be one of the safest ways to save everyone.

If the party begins moving grain to make that option more attractive, Harl may cross from selective truth into a direct lie.

That should be a sign of his desperation, not a general invitation for the referee to make him arbitrarily deceptive.

## Let physical solutions work

A convincing brace holds.

A rope tied to something strong behaves like a rope.

A heavy sack is heavy.

Water flows downhill.

Breaking machinery has consequences.

Do not turn reasonable engineering into repeated ability saves.

Use saves when failure would result from danger or uncertainty, not merely because an action matters.

## Telegraph consequences

Before an irreversible action, make its likely consequence apparent when the characters could reasonably know it.

Examples:

> The gate shifts against your pry bar. If you pull Harl free now, nothing else appears to be holding it.

> The wheel is already shaking violently. With the coupling removed, you could probably open the sluice farther, but there is no reason to expect the wheel to survive.

> The beaver retreats into the nest. To reach the obstruction you'll have to tear directly into its shelter.

> Harl watches the grain sacks disappear upstairs and becomes visibly more agitated.

The players should be making informed compromises.

## Do not protect the intended dilemma

If the players invent a genuinely excellent solution that saves Harl, the grain, the beaver, and the mill, let them have it.

The scenario is not successful because every run produces suffering.

It is successful if the world presents conflicting pressures strongly enough that avoiding all losses requires insight, resources, or risk.

## Let NPCs disagree

Harl is not a quest marker.

Villagers may care more about:

* relatives in the low houses;
* the grain;
* Harl;
* the mill.

Once the sacrificial option becomes known, some villagers may openly disagree with Harl.

Their disagreement should create pressure, not veto player agency.

# Cairn Rules in Play

The scenario deliberately needs almost no special rules.

### STR saves

Appropriate when:

* holding a shifting beam;
* controlling something under heavy load;
* dragging someone through strong current;
* performing strenuous work while immediate failure matters.

### DEX saves

Appropriate when:

* crossing wet machinery;
* avoiding a sudden rush of water;
* moving past the beaver in confined space.

### WIL saves

Use sparingly.

Talking sensibly to Harl does not require one.

A WIL save may make sense when maintaining nerve under frightening circumstances or resisting panic, but persuasion should primarily follow from what is said and what the NPC wants.

### Combat

Attacks hit automatically as usual.

That makes fighting the beaver a deliberately serious option rather than a harmless skill check.

Its bite is dangerous enough to matter without turning it into a boss monster.

### Equipment

Characters who brought useful ordinary equipment should benefit from it.

Rope, hooks, poles, axes, lanterns, food, blankets, and similar items may dramatically change what is possible.

This is intended.

---

# Ending

The scenario ends when the immediate flood danger is controlled and Harl's fate is settled.

There is no required final encounter.

Possible endings include:

* Harl rescued, gate cleared, mill intact;
* Harl rescued, beaver killed;
* Harl rescued, significant grain lost;
* Harl rescued after the party destroys the wheel against his wishes;
* Harl ultimately agrees to sacrifice the wheel;
* grain saved, mill destroyed;
* mill preserved, grain lost;
* flood damages part of the village;
* Harl dies while the players save the mill;
* dam fails after too much delay;
* an unexpected player solution prevents almost all losses.

The aftermath should reflect exactly what happened.

If Harl exaggerated or lied about the danger of sacrificing the mill and this becomes known, villagers may judge him differently depending on the result.

If everyone survives and the mill survives, few may care.

If people or grain were lost while he concealed a faster option, his decision may become a serious grievance.

No hidden moral judgment is required.

The story of the scenario is simply the account of what the characters learned, what they believed, and what they decided was worth risking.

# Referee Summary

**Problem:** Rising millpond.

**Cause:** Beaver nest jams the waste gate.

**Miller's response:** Route more water through the mill.

**Failure:** Millrace gate-control mechanism breaks and pins him.

**Immediate danger:** Moving him carelessly opens the sluice fully.

**Growing dangers:** Dam erosion, flooding mill, ruined grain, worsening injury.

**Proper solution:** Restore the waste gate, reduce pond level, secure mill sluice, rescue Harl.

**Obstruction:** Territorial beaver defending its nest.

**Concealed alternative:** Disconnect and sacrifice the waterwheel so substantially more water can pass through the millrace.

**Why Harl conceals it:** He believes the mill can still be saved and fears losing his livelihood.

**Important emergent strategy:** Move the grain to safety, then free Harl and sacrifice the mill.

**Harl's response:** He first argues, then exaggerates, and may ultimately lie about downstream flood risk to stop the party from choosing that plan.

**Major alternatives:**
Secure the broken gate; lure or kill the beaver; spend grain; move grain; sacrifice the wheel; improvise another water outlet.

**Core question:**
Not “How do we solve the mill puzzle?”

It is:

**What are you willing to risk or sacrifice while trying to save everything—and whose judgment do you trust when their interests are on the line?**
